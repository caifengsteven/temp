High-Frequency Volatility Estimation With Fast Multiple Change Points Detection∗∗ El Mehdi Ainasse †2, Greeshma Balabhadra †2, and Pawe l Polak ‡ 1,2,3 1Center of Excellence in Wireless and Information Technology, Stony Brook University, USA 2 Department of Applied Mathematics and Statistics, Stony Brook University, USA 3Institute for Advanced Computational Science, Stony Brook University, USA Abstract We propose a method for constructing sparse high-frequency volatility estimators that are robust against change points in the spot volatility process. The estimators we propose are ℓ1-regularized versions of existing volatility estimators. We focus on power variation estimators as they represent a fundamental class of volatility estimators. We establish consistency of these estimators for the true unobserved volatility and the change points locations, showing that minimax rates can be achieved for particular volatility estimators. The new estimators utilize the computationally efficient least angle regression algorithm for estimation purposes, followed by a reduced dynamic programming step to refine the final number of change points. In terms of numerical performance, these estimators are not only computationally fast but also accurately identify breakpoints near the end of the sample, both features highly desirable in today’s electronic trading environment. In terms of out-of-sample volatility prediction, our new estimators provide more realistic and smoother volatility forecasts, outperforming a broad range of classical and recent volatility estimators across various frequencies and forecasting horizons. E-mail address: pawel.polak@stonybrook.edu. 1 arXiv:2303.10550v3 [q-fin.ST] 30 Jun 2024 1 Introduction The detection of sudden shifts in volatility has been extensively studied (Malik, 2003, Wang and Moore, 2009, Kumar and Maheswaran, 2013, and references therein). While some of this work has focused on the detection of structural breaks in volatility for particular classical time-series models such as ARCH and GARCH (Kokoszka and Teyssi´ ere, 2002, Mikosch and St˘ aric˘ a, 2004, Rapach and Strauss, 2008, Shi and Ho, 2015, Li et al., 2021, Hu and Gangopadhyay, 2023, and references therein), a lot of work on detecting change points in the volatility of (financial) time series has been more general (Chen and Gupta, 1997, H¨ ardle and Tsybakov, 1997, Wang and Zivot, 2000, Mercurio and Spokoiny, 2004, Pitarakis, 2004, Lavielle and Teyssiere, 2007; and more recently, Mohr and Neumeyer, 2021, and Todorov and Zhang, 2023). Notably, Ross (2013) showed that modeling financial volatility as a process with structural breaks gives an improved fit compared to commonly used parametric volatility modeling techniques. Detecting changes in volatility has significant applications from a risk perspective (Spokoiny, 2009) but also in terms of asset allocation (Nystrup et al., 2016). Under the assumption of no arbitrage, it is known that the price process of an asset is a semi-martingale (Delbaen and Schachermayer, 1994). Specifically, the dynamics of the log-price process are given by an Itˆ o diffusion in which the volatility processσ is typically assumed to be c` adl` ag. Working in this context, we specifically consider the problem of detecting change points in the spot volatility of an Itˆ o semi-martingale. This problem has been treated in the literature to detect a single change-point (Iacus and Yoshida, 2012, Bibinger et al., 2017, Li and Xing, 2022). We aim to offer a data-driven approach to detect multiple change points without assuming a particular parametric form for the volatility process. Recent literature demonstrates that LASSO-based methods yield remarkable perfor- mance in terms of volatility forecasting (Audrino and Knaus, 2016, Ding et al., 2021, Maciak, 2021, Li et al., 2022, Christensen et al., 2022). Using LASSO for change-point detection (Harchaoui and L´ evy-Leduc, 2010, Tibshirani, 2014), we aim to not only detect 2 change points but also propose spot variance estimators that consistently estimate the true underlying variance process. Although our work can be widely applied to various volatility proxies, much of our exposition (particularly in the empirical section) will focus on the LASSO filtering of volatility proxies based on power variation estimators for integrated variance. This choice is motivated by the ease of exposition (as power variation estimators are heavily used in practice), by the results in Liu et al. (2015) who show that 5-minute Realized Variation (RV) is a benchmark that is hard to outperform by any other estimator in a statistically significant way, and by our empirical results which show that the jump ro- bust extension—Bipower Variation (BV) from Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2004)—is the best-performing estimator. Although our estimator is piecewise constant in the simplest case (i.e. assuming that the volatility itself is of finite total variation), there exist numerous volatility estimators constructed under piecewise stationarity assumptions on the volatility that achieve high forecast accuracy (long-term and short-term) compared to classical parametric estimators – see Mercurio and Spokoiny (2004), St˘ aric˘ a and Granger (2005), Davies et al. (2012), Fried (2012), Fryzlewicz et al. (2006), Ross (2013), Chin et al. (2016), Korkas and Fryzlewicz (2017), Chen et al. (2018), Guterding and Boenkost (2018) and references therein. In the same vein as this literature, we also report improvement in volatility forecasting in com- parison to parametric estimators (e.g., GARCH-(1 , 1), FIGARCH, and realized GARCH), despite piecewise constancy of our LASSO-based estimators. Unlike the existing literature, however, we propose a simpler data-driven approach that does not rely on complex modeling methods. Additionally, we do not propose a particular spot volatility estimator but rather a method for detecting change points in any particular volatility proxy, and we show that the resulting filtered volatility proxy is itself a consistent estimator enjoying superior forecasting properties. As in Harchaoui and L´ evy-Leduc (2010), we recast the multiple change-point estimation problem as a variable selection problem: we use the Least Angle Regression (LARS) algorithm from Efron et al. (2004), and the Dynamic Programming (DP) step to implement the Least Squares Total Variation (LSTV ∗) algorithm for breakpoint location detection, and simultaneous estimation of the latent spot volatility process. The theory 3 for the proposed estimators is developed, assuming the potential presence of jumps in the price process and microstructure noise. We provide fairly general results, both asymptotic and non-asymptotic, regarding the ℓ2-consistency of the proposed LASSO-filtered estima- tors in terms of fit. We also propose asymptotic results for the consistency of the change points. In particular, we do not necessarily assume that the jumps in the prices or that the microstructure noise is normally distributed. The main contributions of our paper consist of developing a new class of estimators for the high-frequency spot volatility process that results from a change-point filtering al- gorithm. Our algorithm uses discrete samples of a volatility proxy as the observations. There are numerous proxies in the literature, but we essentially categorize them as proxies based on integrated volatility and proxies based on kernels. We show that the LASSO volatility estimator inherits the theoretical guarantees for the consistency of breakpoint lo- cation and volatility estimation from the consistency of the original high-frequency volatility proxy without structural breaks. By doing so, we extend the consistency results proposed by Harchaoui and L´ evy-Leduc (2010) to the given latent process—the spot volatility of high-frequency returns—and we prove it under more general sub-Weibull distribution as- sumptions on the log-price jump process and the microstructure noise. Empirically, we show that the forecasting performance of the proposed estimator is much better than a broad spectrum of different volatility models often used in practice for all of the data frequencies, as well as one-step-ahead and daily forecasting horizon periods. In particu- lar, the proposed high-frequency volatility estimator outperforms classical QV and BV by Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2004); more recent refinements such as realized volatility and bipower variation (RVB) and realized volatility and quarticity (RVQ) by Yu (2020); the heavy-tailed t-GARCH model with Student- t innovations; its long-memory extension —thet-FIGARCH model from Shi and Ho (2015); as well as the recent TGUH approach to change point detection in high-frequency volatility by Fryzlewicz (2018); and, finally, the mixed-frequency approach—the HAR model from Corsi (2009), where the HAR model is used only for multi-step (daily) volatility forecast. Third column: auto-correlation functions of the forecasted volatilities. Next, to assess the forecasting ability of the proposed estimators, we compare them to various benchmarks. We conduct a rolling window exercise, in which we estimate our models and generate one-step-ahead forecasts for different data frequencies. Specifically, for a given window of data consisting of observations r1,n,r 2,n,··· ,rt,n, we aim to forecast the spot volatility term one-step-ahead matching the frequency of the input data, as well as the integrated variance over one-day-ahead period. Formally, to calculate the forecasts, we use the falling factorial basis from Wang et al. (2014) and form the predictions at arbitrary points as derived in Ramdas and Tibshi- rani (2016). We estimate the piecewise constant component of the volatility. Hence, the falling factorial basis prediction reduces to extrapolating the last estimated volatilitybσ(t/n) 34 obtained from the corresponding model within a given rolling window to t + 1 for our high- frequency estimators. We consider a broad spectrum of volatility estimators commonly used in practice as benchmarks. Starting from the classical QV and BV estimators introduced by Barndorff- Nielsen and Shephard (2004), we also include more recent refinements, such as the realized volatility and bipower variation (RVB) and realized volatility and quarticity (RVQ) estima- tors proposed by Yu (2020). In addition, we consider the heavy-tailedt-GARCH model with Student-t innovations and its long-memory extension, the t-FIGARCH model, introduced by Shi and Ho (2015). We also considered kernel-based volatility estimators introduced by Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2008). Finally, we include the mixed-frequency approach of the HAR-RV model proposed by Corsi (2009). However, the HAR-RV model can only be a benchmark for multi-step (daily) volatility forecasts. Regarding truncation-based estima- tors mentioned in Section 4, we investigated the forecasting performance of MinRV/MedRV estimators proposed by Andersen et al. (2012), and they performed worse than considered power estimators. Hence, they are omitted from the final comparison results. If there are no change points, the LSTV∗(QV) and LSTV∗(BV) estimators reduce to the original QV and BV estimators, respectively. The forecasting exercises below set Kmax = 1 and ξ = 0.3. Allowing for one change point improves forecasting performance across all models, assets, and frequencies considered below,ξ = 0.3 is as in Harchaoui and L´ evy-Leduc (2010). We also experimented with Kmax > 1 and different ξ values—higherKmax require careful selection of ξ. In general, the user should decide on the appropriate choice of Kmax and ξ based on market regimes that she observes in her data and specific applications of the estimated volatility. In Figure 4, we compare rolling window-based one-step-ahead forecasted volatilities and log forecasted volatilities of Apple’s 60-minute frequency returns using BV, the realized RV- GARCH by Hansen and Lunde (2011), and LSTV∗(BV) models. The forecasted volatilities for the BV model exhibit many spikes across consecutive rolling windows, while the realized RV-GARCH produces forecasts with few spikes. By contrast, our proposed model provides much smoother and more realistic forecasts, as seen from their auto-correlation structure. 35 We can use a simple exponential-moving average filter to smooth the forecasts further. The bottom plots in Figure 4 show the results of applying this filter, with the parameter λ chosen to minimize the overall squared error. The proposed estimator, although piecewise constant in-sample, when looking at forecasts from consecutive rolling windows, preserves more stylized facts of a typical volatility process out-of-sample than more flexible parametric models encompassing the same stylized facts in their dynamics in-sample. If volatility forecasting is the main goal, preserving stylized facts, out-of-sample arguably should be considered more important than the flexibility of the in-sample fit, and our parsimonious model allows us to achieve that. For example, one can filter jumps from the stock prices separately from the volatility estimation by adding 40 the ℓ1 regularization term to the objective function in (3). Similarly, intraday or weekly seasonality, a well-known statistical characteristic of high- frequency stock volatility, can be incorporated into our ℓ1 volatility filter. One approach for incorporating seasonality is to follow Vatter et al. (2015) and incorporate smooth trend and seasonality into the framework. To capture both effects, the objective function (3) can be modified as follows: 1 n ˜ϑt− Xβ− s− v 2 2 +λ∥β∥1 +γ∥v∥1 s.t. st+p =sT t = 1,...,n −p, where s = (s1,...,s n)′ ∈ Rn is the seasonal periodic component with known period p, vector v∈ Rn is the sparse spikes component, and the parameter γ≥ 0 controls jumps intensity. If the exact seasonality pattern needs to be estimated from the data, one can use 1 n ˜ϑt− Xβ− s− v 2 2 +λ∥β∥1 +γ∥v∥1 + X ω∈Ω (|aω| +|bω|) s.t. st = X ω∈Ω aω sin(ωt) +bω cos(ωt) t = 1,...,n. The s = (s1,...,s n)′∈ Rn filter is responsible for selecting the best frequencies from an over-complete dictionary Ω. In this case, the frequency does not need to be known a priori, and the penalty term discourages greedily using the dictionary. For an adaptive version of this filter and a fast coordinate descent algorithm for estimation, see Souto et al. (2016). Secondly, one of the assumptions in our analysis so far is that the stock returns are equally spaced because the data used in Section 5 consists of TAQ data aggregated to 1, 5, 10, 30, and 60-minute intervals. We can extend our method to tick-level data, which is irregularly spaced. First, consider the estimation of the underlying continuous volatility from a finite number of data points. Equation (3) can be extended to a continuous form as 41 follows: 1 n nX i=1 (ϑi−xti)2 +λ Z tn t1 | ˙x| dt. This can be simplified by using the standard interpolation problem as explained in Kim et al. (2009). In particular, we can solve 1 n nX i=1  ˜ϑi−xti 2 +λ nX i=2 xti+1−xti ti+1−ti . Using the optimal points xti, i = 1,...,n , we can recover the solution to the original piecewise linear trend filtering problem: the piecewise-linear function, x∗ T , for x∗ ti, given by x∗ T = ti+1−t ti+1−ti x∗ i, where i = 1,...,n , is the optimal piecewise constant volatility. To reflect the non-equally spaced data, one can modify the proposed LSTV∗ algorithm by modifying the matrix X to include the time increments from the regularization term. Alternatively, one can use the modified version of the primal-dual algorithm described in Kim et al. (2009). In this paper, we assumed that the observed returns are the true returns. In ultra-high- frequency data, one needs to accommodate the assumption of microstructure noise. We can do that by using a realized kernel estimator FK(rT ), where K stands for the specific kernel function (see Barndorff-Nielsen et al., 2008, 2009, 2011a,b, and Lunde et al., 2015). Indeed, if we have p(t) = p∗(t) +ϵ(t), where p(t) denotes the observed price at t, p∗(t) denotes the true price att, andϵ denotes the micro-structure noise; then the returns satisfy rT =r∗ T +uT, where uT =ϵ(t)−ϵ(t− 1), and one can construct a kernel-based estimator FK that satisfies p- lim n→+∞ FK(rT ) = [r∗ T ]and p- lim n→+∞ =FK(uT ) = 0, where rT is the vector of high frequency returns; as before, [ r∗ T ] is the quadratic variation of the true returns; and uT = (u1,··· ,un). Finally, the estimators proposed in this paper allow the construction of an estimator of the jump component. Namely, one can take the differences between increments of the QV 42 and BV estimator and plug them into the objective function with ℓ1 regularization not on the difference but on the series itself, i.e., bu(λ) := argmin u∈Rn−1 L(u) (7) whereL(u) := 1 n ˜FRV(rT )−FBP(rT )− u 2 2 +λ∥u∥1, where ˜FRV is the original FRV with the first element r2 1,t in the vector removed. The bu(λ) in (7) estimates the jump component of the price process. They can be obtained via the original LARS or any other LASSO-related algorithm. Since ˜FRV(rT )−FBP(rT ) satisfies our consistency condition for the jump component, the results in this paper also show that bu(λ) will be consistent for the true jump sizes and their locations. 7 Conclusions In this paper, we have introduced a new family of high-frequency volatility estimators. By leveraging the LSTV change point detection framework and adding ℓ1 regularization to two classical estimators (quadratic variation and bipower variation), we have proposed consistent estimators for volatility estimation and breakpoint detection. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed estimators accurately identify breakpoints and outperform classical and recent high-frequency volatility estimators in out-of-sample volatility predic- tion at all frequencies and forecasting horizons. Our proposed estimators offer practical advantages, such as their ability to accurately identify breakpoints close to the end of the sample and provide more realistic and smoother volatility forecasts that accurately capture the stylized facts of volatility when looking at consecutive out-of-sample predictions.
