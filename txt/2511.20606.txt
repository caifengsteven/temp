E-mail(s): wuyao@westlake.edu.cn; Abstract Conventional models of matching markets often assume that compensatory transfers (e.g., bride price) can clear markets by offsetting utility differentials. However, empirical evidence suggests that such transfers frequently fail to close the “psychological gap” between partners, leading to persistent inefficiencies. This paper proposes a market microstructure framework that formalizes mate selection not as a bargaining game, but as a Limit Order Book (LOB) system characterized by rigid bid–ask spreads. We model individual preferences using a Latent Preference State Matrix (LPSM), where the decision rule is driven by the structural spread between an agent’s internal ask price (Unconditional Max) and the market’s best available bid (Reachable Max). We formally demonstrate that this Internal Preference Differential (∆V ) functions as a structural liquidity constraint that is immune to linear monetary compensation . ∆V =Vuncond −Vreach (1) Specifically, we prove aThreshold Impossibility Theorem: any compensation C sufficient to close ∆V must necessarily induce a discontinuous categorical shift in the partner’s identity, effectively bypassing the spread rather than closing it. This implies that financial transfers function as participation fees rather than utility substitutes in the presence of rigid spreads. We further introduce a dynamic discrete choice model where commit- ment execution occurs when the market-to-book ratio θ crosses a time-decaying liquidity thresholdT : θ = Vreach Vuncond ≥T(t). (2) This framework establishes a structural isomorphism between matching failures and liquidity droughts, and mathematically maps post-match regret toexecu- tion slippage. Numerical equilibrium analyses recover key empirical regularities, 1 arXiv:2511.20606v2 [q-fin.TR] 26 Nov 2025 including the inefficiency of transfers in low-tier matches and the invariance of preference orderings under varying regional price norms. The results suggest that matching markets operate asilliquid order-driven systems where execution is constrained by inventory risk and reference-dependent spreads rather than price equilibrium. However, empirical evidence from high-friction matching markets contradicts this monotonicity assumption. These inefficiencies suggest that matching is not merely a price-mediated negotiation but a process constrained by structural illiquidity and reference-dependent spreads , analogous to wide bid–ask spreads in thinly traded asset markets [4, 5]. This paper proposes a market microstructure frameworkthat formalizes mate selection as a Limit Order Book (LOB) system [6]. We argue that the persistent failure of compensation to clear certain matches stems from an internal preference differential—a structural gap between an agent’s internal reference price (what they believe is attainable) and the best available market bid. This aligns with behavioral findings on reference-dependent utility [7, 8]. Unlike price gaps in standard goods markets, this differential acts as a rigid bid– ask spread derived from ordinal rankings [9]. Specifically, the agent’s evaluation operates on a two-sided Attribute Matrix, defining a spread ∆V : ∆V =Vuncond−Vreach. (3) Here,Vuncond represents the agent’s internal Ask Price (Reservation Value based on counterfactuals), and Vreach represents the market’s best Bid Price (Reality). We formally demonstrate that this spread is structurally immune to linear compensation. Execution (marriage) occurs only when the market- to-book ratio θ crosses a time-decaying liquidity threshold T : Vreach Vuncond ≥T (t). (4) This framework maps “settling” behavior to the time decay of limit orders due to inventory costs (e.g., age or social pressure) [10]. Consequently, post-match dissat- isfaction is mathematically equivalent to execution slippage—the realized negative spread between the target price and the execution price [11]. The contributions of this paper are threefold: • Theoretical: We establish a formal isomorphism between matching markets and financial Limit Order Books, demonstrating that “psychological gaps” function strictly as liquidity spreads. 3 • Computational: We implement an agent-based Attribute Matrix simulation to quantify the limits of compensatory transfers, extending classical matching theory with microstructure constraints [12]. • Empirical Consistency: We show that the model recovers stylized facts of assor- tative mating—such as the ineffectiveness of transfers in low-tier matches and the persistence of slippage (regret)—viewing them as natural consequences of market microstructure rather than purely sociological phenomena. By reframing the sociological problem of “marrying down” as a financial problem of liquidity-constrained execution , this work provides a unified computational theory for why money cannot always clear markets, and why structural spreads persist despite aggressive pricing signals. 2 The Internal Differential Model This section develops the mathematical core of our framework. We formalize (1) the agent’s valuation structure, (2) the computation of the structural spread (∆ V ), and (3) the Threshold Impossibility Theorem, which proves that linear compensation cannot close spreads derived from ordinal categorization without inducing a regime switch. 2.1 Preliminaries: Valuation Kernels and Attribute Matrices Let a female agent be denoted by F . Let the set of potential male partners in the market be M ={M1,M 2,...,M n}. (5) Each candidate Mi is evaluated by F through a latent valuation kernel: VF (Mi)∈ R+. (6) This valuation represents a scalar compression of the candidate’s row in the population Attribute Matrix (representing income, education, social capital). We define two subsets within the agent’s preference space: 1. Unconditional Option Set (The Ask Side): UF ={Mi :F believes agents like Mi exist in the market}. (7) 2. Reachable Option Set (The Bid Side): RF ={Mi :Mi is explicitly willing to choose F}. (8) Let the maximal valuations in these sets be: Vuncond = max M∈UF VF (M), (9) Vreach = max M∈RF VF (M). (10) 4 In microstructure terms, Vuncond is the agent’s Internal Ask Price (Reservation Price), and Vreach is the Best Available Bid. 2.2 The Structural Spread (∆ V ) We define the market friction as the Internal Preference Differential: ∆V =Vuncond−Vreach. (11) This ∆V functions as a rigid Bid–Ask Spread: • If ∆V = 0: The market clears at the reservation price (Bid ≈ Ask). • If ∆V > 0: There is a Liquidity Deficit . The agent perceives a loss of value (”trading down”), creating execution latency. The effective utility becomes: Ui =VF (Mi) +f(Ci), (12) wheref is a concave utility function representing diminishing returns (f′ > 0,f′′ < 0). Crucially, the internal ask price Vuncond is invariant to external bids . It is defined relative to the hypothetical ”ideal” asset class. Thus, the effective spread after compensation is: ∆V (C) =Vuncond− (Vreach +f(C)). (13) Since compensation is merely an additive term on the bid side, it does not alter the intrinsic valuation kernel VF (Mi). Therefore: ∆V (C) = ∆V ∀C <C⋆, (14) where C⋆ is the Regime Switching Threshold. 2.4 The Regime Switching Threshold C⋆ We define the critical threshold: C⋆ =Vuncond−VF (Mi). (15) IfMi offersC≥C⋆, thenUi≥Vuncond. However, this condition implies a discontinuity. Compensation sufficient to close the gap effectively elevates the partner into the upper tier, rendering the compensation redundant as a ”transfer” and redefining it as a signal of status elevation. 2.5 The Threshold Impossibility Theorem We state the central theoretical result regarding market clearing via transfers. 6 Theorem 1 (Threshold Impossibility) For any partner M with intrinsic value VF (M), if the compensation C <C⋆, then the structural spread ∆V remains invariant. The preference ordering is preserved: Ui(C)<V uncond. (16) Only if C≥C⋆ does the agent undergo a categorical regime switch. Thus, linear compensa- tion cannot close a spread derived from ordinal categorization without inducing an identity collapse. 2.5.1 Implications for Market Clearing • Invariance: Financial transfers cannot modify the spread ∆ V within the same asset class. • Non-Monotonicity: Preferences do not respond continuously to price improve- ments; they exhibit step-function behavior at C⋆. • Liquidity Constraint: The psychological gap acts as a hard liquidity constraint that money cannot ”lubricate” unless the amount is transformative [5]. 2.6 Persistent Slippage Post-Execution Because ∆V is structural, even if execution occurs (due to threshold decay, see Sec 3), the spread remains encoded in the system: lim t→∞ f(C(t))→ 0, (17) ∆V (t) = ∆V. (18) This creates Persistent Execution Slippage : the realized utility is permanently below the target price. In the context of marriage, this slippage manifests as long- term dissatisfaction or regret, consistent with the behavior of traders executing at unfavorable prices to clear inventory [10]. 2.7 Zero-Spread Execution in High-Tier Matches For high-tier partners Mtop: VF (Mtop)≈Vuncond. (19) Thus, ∆V ≈ 0 and C⋆ ≈ 0. This implies that high-tier matches are Zero-Spread Executions, requiring no price improvement ( C = 0). This explains the empirical regularity that the most stable matches often involve zero compensation, mirroring ”at-the-market” trades in highly liquid assets. 3 Dynamic Execution and Liquidity Thresholds While Section 2 formalized the static structure of the spread ∆ V , real matching deci- sions unfold dynamically over time [12]. Agents do not make a single one-shot decision; instead, execution thresholds evolve as the order book updates and inventory costs 7 (e.g., age) accumulate [13]. In this section, we formalize the matching process as a dynamic discrete choice model . 3.1 States, Transitions, and the Search Process Let the agent F exist in one of three macro-states: S∈{Ssearch, Sevaluate, Sexecute}. (20) 3.1.1 State definitions • Ssearch (Open Order): The agent is active in the market, scanning the order book. The candidate set updates stochastically. • Sevaluate (Price Discovery): The agent narrows the candidate set and computes the market-to-book ratio ( θ). This is equivalent to checking if a limit order is marketable [5]. • Sexecute (Commitment): The trade is executed (marriage). This is an absorbing state unless significant external shocks trigger a ”regime switch” (divorce). Transitions are governed by a time-varying Liquidity Threshold T (t). 3.2 The Liquidity Threshold T (t) We define the decision threshold T ∈ (0, 1], representing the agent’s urgency to execute. In microstructure terms, this reflects the inventory holding cost [10]. Factors influencing T include: • Inventory Age (increases urgency→ lowersT ). • Market Volatility (uncertainty increases T ). • Peer Pressure (social signals act as inventory cost shocks). T evolves according to a decay function: Tt+1 =g(Tt, inventory cost, volatility), (21) where ∂g ∂t < 0, implying that limit orders become more aggressive (lower ask) over time to ensure execution. 3.3 The Execution Condition At evaluation time t, the agent computes the Market-to-Book Ratio: θt = Vreach(t) Vuncond(t) (22) This ratio θt measures how close the best market bid is to the internal reservation price. 8 State: Search (Ssearch) Update Order Book State: Evaluate (Sevaluate) Computeθt = Vreach Vuncond Liquidity Check: θt≥Tt? (24) Mechanism: The agent executes not because the spread closed, but because the liquidity threshold Tt decayed sufficiently to accept the current bid. This explains the phenomenon of ”settling” not as a preference shift, but as a rational response to liquidity constraints. Compensation (C) is not needed to close ∆ V ; rather, time decay lowers the barrier to execution. 3.5 Immediate Execution in High-Tier Matches If the agent encounters a top-tier bid Mtop, then Vreach≈ Vuncond, implying θt≈ 1. SinceTt≤ 1 by definition, the conditionθt >T t is satisfied instantaneously. Therefore: Ssearch → Sevaluate → Sexecute occurs within a single time step. This models the immediate fill (marketable limit order) observed in high-compatibility matches [6]. 9 3.6 Persistent Slippage Post-Execution Post-execution: • Compensation utility f(C) dissipates (one-time transfer). • The structural spread ∆V remains encoded. • The internal ask Vuncond persists as a reference point. Thus, the ratio θt < 1 persists. This implies that the trade was executed at a price below the initial target, resulting inpermanent execution slippage. In social terms, this manifests as ”post-marital dissatisfaction” or regret [11]. 3.7 External Shocks and Repricing (Volatility) Post-execution, external information arrival (e.g., observing a peer’s superior match) can act as a market shock, shifting the internal ask price upward: Vuncond(t + 1)>V uncond(t). (25) Assuming the bid Vreach (current partner) is fixed (illiquid), the ratio θ drops sharply. If θt+1 <T t+1, the agent enters a state of hidden volatility or dissatisfaction. This models the dynamic instability of matches exposed to social comparison shocks. 3.8 Summary of the Dynamic Model The core logic is: 1. Spread Persistence: The internal spread ∆V is structural and immune to linear compensation unless C≥C⋆. 2. Threshold Execution: Decisions are driven by the liquidity threshold T (t), not just price maximization. 3. Time Decay: As inventory costs rise (age),T (t) decays, facilitating execution even with positive spreads (”settling”). 4. Immediate Fills: High-quality bids (θ≈ 1) trigger instant execution. 5. Slippage: Execution below the ask (θ< 1) results in persistent structural regret. 6. Volatility Shocks: External information reprices Vuncond, destabilizing the equi- librium. 4 Numerical Analysis of Execution Dynamics To evaluate the implications of the Internal Differential Model and the dynamic exe- cution rules, we conduct a series of numerical experiments. By formalizing individual choice as a function of internal counterfactuals (Ask Price) rather than external market prices, this model reveals the deeper structural constraints of non-clearing markets. 6.1 Insight 1: Money Cannot Close Structural Spreads Traditional TU models assume that utility deficits can be arbitraged away by transfers. Our model demonstrates that compensation has zero marginal effect on the ordinal 14 ranking of assets: A<B =⇒ A +C̸≻B (if C <C⋆) (27) unless the transfer reaches a Regime Switching ThresholdC⋆. This resolves the empir- ical puzzle of why high premiums fail to clear low-tier inventory. The answer lies in the discrete nature of the asset class: compensation modifies the transaction price, but the asset quality is governed by the counterfactual baseline. Thus, transfers act as liquidity facilitators, not utility substitutes. 6.2 Insight 2: Execution is Driven by Threshold Decay We reframe the concept of ”settling” not as a change in preferences, but as a rational adjustment of the execution threshold T (t) in response to inventory costs. θ = Vbid Vask ≥T (t) (28) This dynamic explains non-linear phenomena such as the sudden acceleration of matching rates at certain age cohorts and the ”panic selling” behavior observed when liquidity dries up. 6.3 Insight 3: Structural Isomorphism with Limit Order Books The most significant theoretical contribution is the identification of a structural iso- morphism between social matching and financial microstructure (see detailed mapping in Appendix D). • Spread ∆V ≡ Bid–Ask Spread: Represents the cost of immediate execution vs. waiting. • Regret≡ Execution Slippage: The mathematical difference between the target price and the realized price. • Social Shocks≡ Information Arrival: Updates to the internal order book that reprice the Ask side. This implies that matching markets are subject to the same laws of liquidity, volatility, and market depth as financial markets. 6.4 Insight 4: Meso-Scale Constraints (Buckets and Cones) Our framework integrates micro-decisions with meso-scale constraints: • Behavioral Heterogeneity (Appendix E): Agents do not optimize over a continuous distribution but rely on heuristic ”Prior Liquidity Buckets.” This discretization explains rigid pricing regimes. • Structural Scarcity (Appendix F): We model the socio-economic hierarchy as a ”Gini Conical Structure.” This geometric model proves that the available volume of reachable partners shrinks super-linearly as status rises, creating a mathematically necessary Liquidity Drought at the top of the pyramid. 15 6.5 Theoretical Contributions This work provides: 1. A Unified Microstructure Model: Bridging matching theory, decision theory, and market microstructure. 2. Resolution of the Compensation Paradox: Explaining why transfers cannot clear structural spreads without identity transformation. 3. Quantification of Slippage: Providing a mathematical definition for post-match dissatisfaction based on spread analysis. 4. A General Theory of Illiquid Matching: Applicable beyond marriage to labor markets, university admissions, and OTC asset trading. 6.6 Limitations and Extensions Future work can refine several areas: • L1. Network Effects:Explicitly modeling the propagation of informational shocks through a social network graph. • L3. Multi-Period Games: Extending the single-execution model to a repeated game with renegotiation (divorce/remarriage options). 7 Conclusion By formalizing mate selection as a Limit Order Book (LOB) system rather than a cooperative game or a transferable utility market, this paper provides a microstructure explanation for the limits of compensatory transfers. We demonstrate that the internal preference differential ∆ V acts as a structural bid–ask spread that linear price improvement (compensation) cannot bridge without triggering a categorical regime switch. Matches in this system are governed not by market-clearing prices, but by the dynamics of threshold decay and inventory management. The model reveals that post-match dissatisfaction is structurally isomorphic to execution slippage in illiquid markets: the realized spread between the internal Ask and the executed Bid persists due to the invariance of the reference point.Ultimately, we show that execution probabilities are bounded by dual structural con- straints: the geometric Liquidity Drought imposed by the “Gini Cone” (Appendix F) and the rigid Pricing Regimes derived from “Prior Liquidity Buckets” (Appendix E). This unified framework bridges market microstructure and social matching the- ory, offering a rigorous quantitative basis for analyzing non-clearing markets characterized by structural spreads, inventory risk, and reference-dependent execution.
